How to check it yourself

The coolant temperature sensor sits in the thermostat housing or in the cylinder head. It is a two-wire thermistor (NTC): the hotter it gets, the lower its resistance. The ECU uses it to decide how much fuel to give on start, when to switch on the fan, and when to cut power for overheating.

What it should read

Resistance of a typical NTC sensor at 20 °Cabout 2…3 kΩ
The same at 80 °Cabout 300 Ω — more precisely per the factory table
Voltage at the harness connector with the sensor unpluggedabout 5 V
Warm engineusually 80…95 °C

Check order

  1. FMI 0, 15, 16 (temperature high): check the engine first, not the sensor. Coolant level in the expansion tank, belt and fan clutch, radiator clogged with dirt on the outside, tank cap.
  2. Compare the dash reading with an infrared thermometer on the top radiator hose. If they agree — the overheating is real.
  3. FMI 3, 4: unplug the connector and measure the sensor resistance. An open sensor or wire is seen by the ECU as −40 °C frost (voltage high, FMI 3), a short as severe overheating (FMI 4). With the connector unplugged, the harness contacts should show about 5 V.
  4. Compare the diagnostics reading on a cold engine that has stood overnight with other temperatures: air, oil, coolant. All should be close to the outside temperature; one lagging 10 °C or more is suspect.
  5. FMI 1, 17, 18 (not warming up): a thermostat stuck open — especially in winter at idle.

Common causes — from most to least frequent

  1. Real overheating: low coolant, dirty radiator, fan clutch, water pump.
  2. Thermostat stuck open or closed.
  3. Corroded sensor connector.
  4. The sensor itself.
  5. Air lock after a coolant change.

Can I keep driving

Do not drive. Stop as soon as it’s safe to do so — don’t keep going.

What happens if you keep driving

Time you have: Minutes

The engine is already past its temperature limit. An aluminium head goes fast: first the geometry warps, then the gasket burns through.

Next comes a blown head gasket, a damaged head and, at worst, a cracked block. Shut it down and let it cool — never open the expansion tank cap while hot.

This is an electrical fault: the sensor or its wiring is lying, not the cooling system. The temperature gauge is unreliable now and the fan may not kick in on time. Watch the coolant level and keep the engine off load on long climbs.

What to check on the spot

Let the engine cool — never open the expansion tank cap while hot. Then check the level and the hoses.

Which codes follow

If the value really did go out of range, rather than a sensor circuit being broken, these normally light up after it:

  • SPN 1108Engine protection timer override
  • SPN 1569Torque limited (engine protection)

They are not fixed separately — they go out together with this one. The list is deliberately short: only the links the reference is sure about.

What SPN 110 and its FMI tell you

SPN 110 is engine coolant temperature in SAE J1939. The sensor is a thermistor in the cylinder head or the coolant outlet: its resistance falls as it gets hotter. The engine computer uses it to switch the fan on, to derate power and, in the worst case, to shut the engine down.

  • FMI 0 - temperature above normal, most severe (Cummins SID 151). The engine is overheating: stop.
  • FMI 15 and 16 - above normal, least and moderately severe. A warning first, then a power reduction.
  • FMI 3 - circuit voltage high, i.e. an open circuit (Cummins 144). The computer thinks the engine is cold, so protection and fan control may be wrong.
  • FMI 4 - circuit voltage low or shorted to ground (Cummins 145). The computer thinks the engine is overheating.
  • FMI 2 - the reading jumps or disagrees with other data.

Causes of a real overheat, most common first

  • Low coolant from a leak at the radiator, hoses, water pump or heater circuit.
  • The fan not engaging: a failed clutch, a drive fault or a relay.
  • A blocked radiator, dirty outside (insects, chaff) or scaled inside.
  • A thermostat stuck closed: the upper radiator hose is hot while the lower one stays cool.
  • A weak radiator cap, or a pump with a worn impeller.
  • A leaking head gasket: bubbles in the expansion tank, oil in the coolant, white smoke. This is the worst case.

How to check

1. Stop and let the engine cool before opening the cap. Check the level in the expansion tank and look for drips.

2. Compare the scan-tool temperature with reality. After a cold night it should equal the ambient temperature. If it does not, the sensor or its circuit is at fault.

3. With the engine warm, listen for the fan engaging and feel the hoses (carefully): hot upper and cold lower hose points to a closed thermostat.

4. For FMI 3 and 4, inspect the sensor connector for corrosion and test the sensor against the manual's resistance table.

Normal operating temperature on most diesels is roughly 80-95 C; check your engine manual for exact limits.

Can you keep driving?

Not with FMI 0 or a red coolant lamp: a few minutes of overheating can warp the head. Pull over and let the engine cool. With FMI 15 or 16, find a safe place and diagnose; driving on with a climbing gauge is how head gaskets fail. With FMI 3 or 4 the engine may be fine, but the over-temperature protection is blind, so watch the dash gauge closely.

Questions and answers

It only overheats on hills. Is that SPN 110?

The code is only the reporter. The cause is usually a radiator or fan that cannot keep up under load: check the fins, the fan clutch and the thermostat.

The gauge reads far too cold or too hot right after starting. Why?

That points to an open or shorted sensor circuit (FMI 3 or 4). Check the connector and wiring, then the sensor.

Can I top up with plain water to get home?

Only as an emergency measure to reach the nearest safe stop, and only after the engine has cooled. Plain water lowers corrosion protection and the boiling point margin.
